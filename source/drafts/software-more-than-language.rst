Software is more than its languages
===================================

For a while now, I've been toying with various points of view about software,
like its relation to natural languages like English or Portuguese,
how different programming languages have different "tastes",
or how programming feels more like an art expression rather than a mechanical, rule-follwing task,
or its (very) apparent relation to mathematical concepts like sets that pop-up everywhere,
or even how a written program is nothing but an embodiment of a given specification.

In various ways, if it wasn't really clear up to this point, I like programming.
I think I understand a bit about it, but I'd like to understand more.


Translation Layer
-----------------

One of the ways that I see programming is by the lens of a "translation activity",
from a given specification to a concrete, complete and fully defined program.

(INSERT QUOTE HERE) that says: ~"once you ship your program, undefined behavior is relied upon, and thusly becomes defined".

That's to say, that even if something is not specified or not implemented in the program,
by the nature of just existing it has now become defined.

If that definition is something that is intended, beneficial or even desired, is a completely different story.

Being a translation operation, the maxim "garbage-in -> garbage out" is completely valid.
Now, we only have to fully define what's "in".


Arts and Crafts
---------------

On some way that I can't yet fully explain, programming is like arts and crafts.
Its a way to express yourself, be creative and try out new ideas.


Errors
------

Software errors come in all shapes and sizes:

    - Syntax erros
    - Compile-time errors, runtime errors
    - Logic errors
    - Data representation errors
    - Architetural errors

This list can be seen as well as a "line gradient",
where the top items are local, mechanical-like errors
and the bottom ones are more global, decision-derived errors.

That's to say:

    - Syntax errors (read: missing `;`) are easily detectable, easily fixable, impact one developer in his own work
      and are usually the result of mishaps or typos,
    - On the other hand, architectural errors impact a fuckload of people, are not easily fixable
      and are usually the result of bad decisions.

Abstract Thought
----------------

I have the impression that "an exceptional programmer" is kinda like also a philosofer.
I extend the same courtesy (for a lack of a better word) to the mathematicians.
My inclination for that is because every now and again I catch myself thinking in abstract terms, like "what is the relationship between Entity A and Entity B?"
Or "how is it best to describe the fact that Entity A knows about Entity B, can interact with it, but doesn't really know much more?".

Why everybody should learn it
-----------------------------

For a long time I've defended the idea that everybody should learn how to program.
And the reason is pretty simple: it's not that hard and it greatly benefits the learner.

Furthermore, if one considers a programming language to be analogous to a foreign language (e.g. Italian), what's the "actual" challenge?
Like, people learn other languages, and the languages become part of the personality of the person.
They help shape the way one expresses, widens the universe that one oberves, presents different thoughts on things
and provides a culture with which the person can act within.

However, the reader should not confuse the statement with "everybody should be proficient with it".
It's no fun when it becomes like chemestry in grade 4: "today we are going to learn about covalent bonds".
I know the name to this day, but I have no bloody idea of what the actual fuck it describes.
Even worse, I can't run any experiment in my office to discover it myself!
What good is that?
